Aviation learned, at great cost, how to hand an instruction from one person to another so that it cannot be misheard. rogerwilco gives that discipline to AI agents.

On 27 March 1977 two Boeing 747s collided on a runway in Tenerife and 583 people died. One crew began its take-off on a clearance it had not been given. The words on the radio were ordinary English, and ordinary English was the problem: "we are now at take-off" meant one thing to the crew saying it and another to the controller hearing it. Aviation's answer was not a better pilot. It was a protocol. A fixed vocabulary, so a word means one thing. The person who will act reads the instruction back, and the person who gave it listens for the mistake. In the flight deck, the setting is called out and the other pilot checks the dial. Flying became the safest way to travel, and that discipline is part of why.

AI agents are now doing things that cannot be undone: paying invoices, changing the account a supplier is paid into, sending emails to customers. They hand each other those instructions in paragraphs. rogerwilco gives them the flight deck's discipline instead: three tiny messages, each a single line of text, every fact with a label, so a program can read it like a form and check it before anything happens.

The names come from the radio. Roger means "I received it". Wilco means "I understood it and I will do it". A controller says "descend to 3,000 feet, QNH 1013"; the pilot says it back; the controller listens. Agents can do the same, and a program can do the listening.

1. Hand over scheduler to payer

"Pay invoice MD-2026-057: 5,400.00 pounds, gross, to account 40-12-88 / 61102938, on 2 October, under approval FD-0929. Read back what you will do before you do anything."

RW1 HO scheduler-1 p=pay from=scheduler to=payer item=inv:MD-2026-057 ask=PAY rb=exec sup=marlow amt=5400.00 cur=GBP kind=GROSS acct=40-12-88/61102938 paid=20261002 ref=MD-2026-057 apref=FD-0929

2. Read back payer to scheduler

"Paying invoice MD-2026-057, 5,400.00 pounds, gross, to 40-12-88 / 61102938, the account on file for Marlow, on 2 October, under FD-0929."

RW1 RB payer-1 re=scheduler-1 from=payer to=scheduler item=inv:MD-2026-057 ask=PAY p=pay h=a7a82de76607 sup=marlow amt=5400.00 cur=GBP kind=GROSS acct=40-12-88/61102938 paid=20261002 ref=MD-2026-057 apref=FD-0929

checkA program compares this line with the first one, label by label. The values are the ones the payer will act with, the account read from its own master, so an instruction that named the wrong account shows up here. h= is a fingerprint of the message as it arrived, so a digit changed on the way shows up too. If anything differs, the payment waits and the scheduler sends the instruction again. If it all matches: roger, wilco.

3. Done payer to scheduler

"Paid 5,400.00 pounds to 40-12-88 / 61102938 on 2 October, reference MD-2026-057, under FD-0929."

RW1 DN payer-2 re=scheduler-1 p=pay from=payer to=scheduler item=inv:MD-2026-057 out=OK sup=marlow amt=5400.00 cur=GBP kind=GROSS acct=40-12-88/61102938 paid=20261002 ref=MD-2026-057 apref=FD-0929

checkThe done message carries the values as they were actually used, not as they were asked for, so a program can check those against the instruction too. That is the second loop: not just "I understood", but "here is what I did". In our sixth study it was the one check that caught a tool doing something other than what it was told, and it is in the rulebook: check-dn.

9 of 16, then 0

Does it carry over? We planted sixteen faults in handovers between agents just before a payment, a bank-detail change and a supplier email: a wrong digit, the wrong account, the wrong date, a message meant for someone else. Without a readback, nine of the sixteen became a wrong act that could not be undone (fourteen with a smaller model). With the line read back and checked by a program, none did, on either model, and no correct handover was wrongly held up. Six small studies so far, so this is an indication rather than proof, and every log is in the open. What we did and what happened, and the four studies before it, including the one that found that with a strong model the lines do not save money. What they save is mistakes.
