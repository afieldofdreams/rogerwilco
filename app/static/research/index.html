Five studiesSeptember to October 2026Croxio Ltd

What we tested, what happened, and what it means

Five studies, one after another, each asking the question the one before it raised. Everything in them is made up for the test: the companies, the invoices, the clinical notes. Every log and every grade is in the repository. Each study is told here in plain words; the full report is one click away.

1. Do the lines save money?

Wire study, 27 September. 240 messages, five ways of writing them, two models.

What we didWe wrote the same 240 handover messages five ways: plain English, JSON, a rogerwilco line, a form made of symbols, and a very tight form where every fact sits in a fixed position. We counted the tokens each way costs, checked that every message survives being turned into data and back, and gave 60 of them to a model that had not seen the format and asked what it would do with each.

What happenedA line costs about 29% fewer tokens than English; the tight form about 59% fewer. Every message survived the round trip. The strong model understood every form perfectly. The small model lost its place in the tight form in one run of two, getting four messages in ten wrong; in the other run, one in twelve.

What it meansThe saving per message is small: 20 to 40 tokens. A hundred handovers a day save less than one resend of a system prompt. So tokens are not the reason to use lines. The reason is that a program can check them. The tight form is for programs talking to programs, not for models.

2. Does reading a message back catch misunderstandings?

Readback pilot, 28 September. 36 handovers, three ways of handing over, two models.

What we didWe gave agents briefs with traps in them. For example, the message said "120 words" but the attached brief said "150 words, no more". Then we handed the job over three ways: in plain English with no readback; in plain English with the receiver writing back what it understood, judged by another model; and as a rogerwilco line with the receiver reading it back and a program checking it.

What happenedWithout a readback, 4 of 36 jobs were done on the wrong reading and stayed wrong. With a readback of either kind, every misread was flagged and fixed with one correction. The line version prevented two of them before any check, probably because having to put a number after len= made the receiver read the brief instead of trusting the message (one trap, two runs: a strong hypothesis, not a result). On the small model the program over-flagged terse readbacks, ten false alarms in eleven; a rule change planned for the next version would remove them.

What it meansReading back works. The line is a little better than a paragraph, mainly because it forces a value. A readback proves the receiver understood; it does not prove it will do it. A small model read a rule back correctly five times and then ignored it. That gap is what the fifth study looks at.

3. What happens when four agents close a company's month together?

Month-end close, 29 to 30 September. 28 runs, six ways of writing messages (two of them rerun with the small model), two scenarios, two models.

What we didFour agents (one reads the emails, one checks the numbers, one builds the payment run, one audits it) closed the September accounts of a made-up company, with a finance director who decides only what the policy reserves for them. We planted things that should be escalated (a bank-detail change from a lookalike email, a payment above the limit, an invoice in dollars) and things that should not (a corrected invoice, a duplicate, a small variance). We ran it with messages in free English, in English under seven rules, as lines, as lines with a full readback, and as lines that replace the working files. One scenario added a phishing instruction hidden in an email and a wrong answer from the boss.

What happenedWith a strong model every way of writing got the payment run right, escalated exactly the three reserved decisions, left the four decoys alone, ignored the phishing paragraph and pushed back on the boss's wrong answer. English written under the seven rules cut the chatter between agents to a third and the director's reading to 40%, as well as the lines did and more cheaply. The lines added what English cannot: a program refused 18 broken or made-up messages from the small model (English has no such check); and when the lines replaced the files the agents pass each other, those files shrank from 22,000 tokens a run to under 2,000 with every fact kept. The small model paid a dollar invoice it should have held: in English in both runs, in lines in one of two.

What it meansThe habits do the saving. The lines do the checking. A strong model gets there on instructions alone; a small model is unreliable in either form, and less so when a program is checking.

4. Can a document become lines of fact you can trust?

Translation study, 30 September. 12 clinical notes, 422 statements marked by hand, 144 translations, two models.

What we didWe wrote twelve made-up clinical notes with the hard things in them: things that are denied, things that might be, things about a relative, things from years ago, a table, a correction, a hidden instruction. We marked every statement by hand: 422 of them. Then we asked two models to turn each note into one line per statement, with a pointer to the exact words in the note, twelve times each. A program refused any line whose words were not in the note at the place it pointed to.

What happenedThe strong model found 99.7% of the statements, pointed at the right words 99% of the time, and invented nothing, because a line without words behind it cannot get through. The small model had 70 of its first 72 attempts refused, mostly for paraphrasing instead of quoting, got every note through in two rounds, and still misread meaning: it marked what the patient said as something the doctor observed 59 times, and read the present as the past 104 times. Writing the same facts as JSON instead of lines made no difference to accuracy or cost.

What it meansThe pointer and the check make the record trustworthy: nothing in it is made up, and every fact can be traced to its words. A weak model is made correct in form, not in meaning.

5. Before something that cannot be undone, does reading back catch a fault?

Readback and set-back, 6 October. 114 trials, 19 handovers, 16 of them with a planted fault, three ways of handing over, two models.

What we didWe handed an agent single jobs it cannot take back: release a payment, change a supplier's bank account, send a supplier an email. Into sixteen of the nineteen handovers we planted one fault: a wrong digit, the wrong account, the wrong date, the wrong invoice, net instead of gross, a message meant for a different agent. Three ways of handing over, as in the pilot: English with no readback; English with a readback judged by a model; a line read back and checked by a program. The agent then did the job and reported what it had done, or asked or refused instead.

What happenedWith no readback, 9 of the 16 faults became a wrong act that could not be undone (14 with the small model). The agent caught the ones it could see for itself, like an account that was not on file, and let the rest through. With an English readback judged by a model, 1 and 3 got through, every one a message addressed to someone else. With a line read back and checked by a program, none got through on either model: every wrong figure was caught at the readback, and the agent refused or queried the misaddressed messages (the program will not build a readback for a message that is not for you, and the strong model said so). No correct handover was wrongly held up. In the lines arm every job done after a confirmed readback was right, and every "done" report told the truth about what had been done.

What it meansFor the step you cannot undo, the line read back and checked by a program is the control. The "done" report with the actual values is the second loop; in this study it had nothing to catch, because nobody did the wrong thing after a correct readback. Two things to fix: a corrected message should say why it changed, and the done report belongs in the rulebook.

The honest summary

Lines do not save money with a strong model; the habits do. Lines let a program check what agents say to each other, refuse made-up words, trace every fact to its source, and catch a wrong figure before a payment goes out. With a small model that matters a lot. With a strong model it matters at the steps you cannot undo. Every study here is small: one to three runs per cell, two models, one author writing both the tests and the marking. Enough to show direction; not yet a measurement.
