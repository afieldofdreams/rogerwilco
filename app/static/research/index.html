What we tested, what happened, and what we decided

Seven studies, one after another, each asking the question the one before it raised. Everything in them is made up for the test: the companies, the invoices, the clinical notes. Every log and every grade is in the repository. Each study is told here in brief; its own page says why we did it, what we tested, how we ran it, what happened, what it means and what we decided, in that order. For the story first, the home page walks through six ways of handing over a job, with a picture of each and what it caught; the map below says which study each of those tests comes from.

1. Does writing a handover as a line save tokens, and can a model still read it?

The wire study, 27 September. 240 messages, five ways of writing them, two models.

WhyThe idea on the table was that agents talking English is wasteful and they need their own language. Before building on that we wanted to know what a fixed-vocabulary line actually saves, whether it loses anything, and whether a model reads it as well as English.

What we didWrote the same 240 handover messages five ways: plain English, JSON, a key=value line, a form made of symbols, and a very tight form where every fact sits in a fixed position. Counted the tokens, checked that every message survives being turned into data and back, corrupted lines to see what the decoder catches, and gave 60 messages in each form to a model that had never seen the format, asking what it would do with each.

What happenedA line costs about 29% fewer tokens than English; the tight form about 59% fewer. Every message survived the round trip. The strong model read every form perfectly. The small model lost its place in the tight form in one run of two, getting four messages in ten wrong.

What we decidedThe saving is 20 to 40 tokens a message, so tokens are not the reason to use lines; what a line gives is something a program can check. The key=value line became the rogerwilco line. Symbols and the positional form were dropped, a corrupted digit is caught by a digest on the readback instead, and no code may be spelled like an answer.

2. Does reading a handover back catch a misunderstanding before the work is done?

The readback pilot, 28 September. 108 handovers, 90 of them with a planted trap, three ways of handing over, two models.

WhyIf lines do not save money, they might make handovers safer: a program can compare what the receiver says it understood with what the sender meant. We wanted to know whether a readback catches a misunderstanding at all, and whether a typed line does it better than a paragraph.

What we didGave agents jobs with traps in the instructions, for example a handover that said 120 words when the attached brief said 150. Handed each job over three ways: plain English with no readback; plain English with the receiver writing back what it understood, judged by another model; and a line with the receiver reading it back and a program comparing it with criteria the sender had kept to itself.

What happenedWithout a readback, 4 of 36 jobs were done on the wrong reading and stayed wrong. With a readback of either kind, every misread that reached an output was caught and fixed with one correction. The line prevented the strong model's misreads before any check ran, because having to put a number after len= sent it to the brief. On the small model the program over-flagged terse readbacks, ten false alarms in eleven. A readback proved understanding, not execution: the small model read one rule back correctly in every readback and then broke it in four of six jobs.

What we decidedNo handover without a readback became rule 1 of the protocol, and the comprehension readback, with a closed set of criterion keys and the sender naming which it will check, went into the spec. The receiver gets a short generated legend instead of the whole skill file, which was nearly all of a line handover's extra cost. A check of the finished work against the same criteria was left open and picked up by the fifth study.

3. When four agents close a company's month, where does the saving come from?

Month-end close, 29 to 30 September. 28 runs, six ways of writing messages, two scenarios, two models.

WhyReal work is a team of agents passing messages and files, with a person who must be asked some things and not others. We wanted the lines in that setting, against the obvious alternative of telling the agents to write well, with a small model in the seats as well as a strong one.

What we didFour agents (one reads the emails, one checks the numbers, one builds the payment run, one audits it) closed the September accounts of a made-up company, with a finance director who decides only what the policy reserves. We planted things that should be escalated (a bank-detail change from a lookalike email, a payment above the limit, an invoice in dollars) and things that should not (a corrected invoice, a duplicate, a small variance). We ran it with messages in free English, in English in three fixed sections, in English under seven rules, as lines, as lines with every fact read back, and as lines that replace the working files. A second scenario added a phishing instruction hidden in an email and a wrong answer from the boss.

What happenedWith the strong model every way of writing got the payment run right, escalated exactly the three reserved decisions, left the four decoys alone, ignored the phishing paragraph and pushed back on the boss's wrong answer. English under the seven rules cut the traffic between agents to a third and the director's reading to 40%, as well as the lines did and more cheaply. The lines added what English cannot: a program refused 18 broken or made-up messages from the small model, and when the lines replaced the files the agents pass each other, those files shrank from 22,000 tokens a run to under 2,000 with every fact kept.

What we decidedThe seven rules lead the skill, because the discipline does the saving; the lines are for where a program must check: small or mixed models, steps that cannot be undone, logs someone has to trust, and the edge where documents enter. The readback level is set per handover, full before an irreversible act and a short echo otherwise. Profiles may close the answer vocabulary. And at the edge, the lines are the record: one line per document with a pointer to its source and no working file.

4. Can a document become lines of fact you can trust?

The translation study, 30 September. 12 clinical notes, 422 statements marked by hand, 144 translations, two models.

WhyThe month-end study ended with lines replacing the working files, one line per email with a pointer to its source. Emails are easy. We wanted to know whether the rule holds where the facts are dense and a wrong reading matters, and whether the notation itself matters against JSON.

What we didWrote twelve made-up clinical notes with the hard things in them: findings that are denied, diagnoses that are only possible, facts about a relative, facts from years ago, a results table, a correction, a hidden instruction. Marked every statement by hand, 422 of them. Asked two models to turn each note into one line per statement, each pointing at the exact words in the note, as lines and as JSON, three runs each: twelve translations of every note. A program refused any line whose words were not in the note at the place it pointed to.

What happenedThe strong model found 99.7% of the statements, pointed at the right words 99% of the time, and invented nothing, because a line without words behind it cannot get through. The small model had 70 of its 72 first attempts refused, got every note through in two rounds, and still misread meaning: it marked what the patient said as something the doctor observed 59 times and read the present as the past 104 times. Lines and JSON came out the same. The record was 5.6 times the size of the note.

What we decidedThe clinical profile and its gate are part of rogerwilco, and the gate is a hosted tool. The claim is structure and provenance, not compression: a record that is bigger than the note is worth having because every line is traceable and nothing in it is made up. Pipelines write one document per call. Binding concepts to a terminology and stating the envelope once per block are the next changes.

5. Before something that cannot be undone, does reading back catch a fault?

Readback and set-back, 6 October. 114 trials, 19 handovers, 16 of them with a planted fault, three ways of handing over, two models.

WhyThe pilot showed a readback catching misunderstandings but not proving execution; the month-end study found nothing for a full readback to catch on a clean run. Neither tested a fault in a handover just before an act that cannot be undone. In aviation there are two loops, the readback and the cross-check of what was set. This study plants faults so both have something to catch.

What we didHanded an agent single jobs it cannot take back: release a payment, change a supplier's bank account, send a supplier an email. Into sixteen of nineteen handovers we planted one fault: a wrong digit, the wrong account, the wrong date, the wrong invoice, net instead of gross, a message meant for a different agent. Three ways of handing over: English with no readback; English with a readback judged by a model; a line read back and checked by a program, followed by a done report carrying the values as executed.

What happenedWith no readback, 9 of the 16 faults became a wrong act that could not be undone (14 with the small model). With an English readback judged by a model, 1 and 3 got through, every one a message addressed to someone else. With a line read back and checked by a program, none got through on either model, and no correct handover was wrongly held up. Every act after a confirmed readback was right, 65 of 65, and every done report told the truth, 28 of 28.

What we decidedFor the step you cannot undo, the line read back and checked by a program is the control, and the rule that requires it stands on this evidence. Readbacks are built by code wherever code exists. Two protocol changes follow and are open: a corrected handover says why it changed, and the done report with executed values goes into the spec as the second loop, with a run that stresses it.

6. Before an act that cannot be undone, does reading back the plan catch what an echo cannot?

The execution readback, 10 and 11 October. 2,762 trials, three fault blocks, five ways of handing over, two Claude models, pre-registered; then a partial run of the same design on two OpenAI receivers, and a free-prose extension.

WhyStudy 5 caught every planted fault by construction: the readback was built by code from the message, so comparing it with the record caught whatever the message carried, which a check before sending would have caught too. The claim to be a safety control rests on two other faults, the receiver reading the handover wrongly and the tool doing something other than it was told. This study was built to test those, with the echo kept in as the arm that should catch nothing there, and its hypotheses, tests and sizing written down before it ran.

What we didHanded the payer correct handovers that name the supplier and the figures but not the account or the address, which it must resolve from a master built to mislead: lookalike rows, superseded and pending accounts, decoy handovers, stale drafts, emails from lookalike domains. Five ways of handing over, from none to an execution readback in which the receiver writes the values it will use and a program checks them against the record. A second block re-ran study 5's planted drafting faults; a third made the tool execute something other than it was asked, and read back what it did.

What happenedNo receiver made a wrong act against a correct handover, in any arm, at any of four levels of hardening: 0 of 140 induced trials per cell on Sonnet 5.5 and Haiku 5.5, 0 of 224 each across the ladder, 0 of 56 on Haiku 4.5. They resolved the trap or asked. gpt-5.5, run afterwards through the API harness, read the same way. gpt-5.4-mini did not: under the echo readback it made 10 wrong acts in 140 induced contexts and 4 in 50 clean ones, after readbacks that had passed; its typed execution readbacks never carried the resolved values and were held; the fixed-shape English block, parsed by code, it completed, and that caught a wrong address and corrected it in four of five trials where the echo let it through. Against the sender's own drafting faults, the no-readback arm made a wrong irreversible act 17 and 14 times in 48, and both readback arms caught every one, as a check before sending does. Against the tool, the done report carrying the tool's printed values let the sender's check flag 45 of 45 injected faults on each receiver, and every report was honest.

What we decidedA handover before an irreversible act is checked against the record before it is sent, because that is where the readback's catches came from. The done report with the executed values goes into the spec as the second loop, because that is where the uncaught faults were. The readback before an irreversible act stays; on the two Claude receivers it had nothing to catch, raised no false alarm, and cost one turn, and on gpt-5.4-mini its form decided everything: the echo passed a wrong plan, the typed execution readback stalled, the parsed English block caught. Whether the spec should accept that block as a form of rb=exec is the open question.

7. Does a readback the tool composes catch what the model's cannot?

Prepare and confirm at the tool, 11 and 12 October. 794 trials on Haiku 5.5 and 1,014 on gpt-5.4-mini, each after a pilot, three fault blocks, six ways of handing over, pre-registered.

WhyEvery readback so far was the model's account of what it meant to do, and nothing bound the tool call that followed to it. Study 6's weak receiver failed in that gap: the readback was right, the call was not. Pilots close the same gap by reading the machine, not each other: the cleared altitude is set in the window and the window is checked. We built the window.

What we didThe payment tool gained two steps. Prepare states exactly what it will execute, freezes it under a token and prints the readback line itself; commit executes that statement and nothing else. Two new arms beside no readback and the model's own execution readback: the tool's statement relayed by the model, and the statement read by the sender from the tool's log with a commit the tool refuses until the sender confirms. Study 6's misleading contexts again, fifteen tool faults injected either when the tool resolves the call or at the moment it executes, and study 5's planted drafting faults as a regression check.

What happenedA fault the tool introduced when it resolved the call became a wrong irreversible act in 29 of 30 trials with no readback and 30 of 30 with the model's execution readback, whose values were right. With the two-phase tool, 0 of 30 in either arm: in every trial Haiku read the statement the tool printed, saw it differed from what it had passed, abandoned and asked, before the sender's check ever ran. Against the misleading contexts no arm made a wrong act, the statement raised no false alarm in 255 checks, the model never altered the line it relayed and never committed early, and the two new arms cost less than composing a readback. A fault injected at the moment of execution got through every arm, by construction, and the done check flagged all of them. Then the weak receiver, gpt-5.4-mini, whose own readbacks fail: under the echo it made 14 wrong acts, under its typed readback it stalled in 52 trials, and under the tool's statement with the commit enforced it made none, was held 7 times, and the sender's check of the statement caught the old sort code and the lookalike address it had passed to the tool. Relaying the statement through that receiver was unreliable in 51 of 129 trials, though it never committed early.

What we decidedWhere the tool can state its transaction before executing it, the execution readback is the tool's statement; a receiver that can be trusted relays it, and otherwise the sender reads the tool. The tool takes the handover by id so the receiver never retypes it. The done check stays as the loop after the act.

The honest summary

Lines do not save money with a strong model; the habits do. Lines let a program check what agents say to each other, refuse made-up words, trace every fact to its source, and catch a wrong figure before a payment goes out. With a small model that matters a lot. With a strong model it matters at the steps you cannot undo, and there the check that earned its place is the tool's own statement of what it is about to do, checked before it does it. Every study here is small: one to three runs per cell, two models, one author writing both the tests and the marking. Enough to show direction; not yet a measurement.
